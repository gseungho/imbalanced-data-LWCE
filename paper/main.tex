\begin{abstract}
Class imbalance is a pervasive obstacle in machine learning, arising whenever a
few majority categories dominate the training data while critically important
minority categories remain severely underrepresented. Among loss-level
remedies, Weighted Cross-Entropy (WCE) is the most direct: it scales the loss of
each class by the inverse of its frequency. However, as the imbalance becomes more severe, these
inverse-frequency weights induce an increasingly large rare-to-frequent weight
ratio, so that minority samples can dominate the class-dependent component of the
gradient scale and destabilize optimization. More elaborate alternatives such as
Class-Balanced (CB) Loss and Focal Loss introduce additional hyperparameters and
can be sensitive to their selection and to dataset characteristics. We propose Log-Weighted Cross-Entropy (LWCE), which
replaces the inverse-frequency weight with a logarithmically compressed
counterpart that keeps a meaningful boost for rare classes while bounding weight
growth, and which introduces no additional hyperparameters. We further generalize
it to Power-Log Weighted Cross-Entropy (PLWCE) through a single exponent that
adjusts the focusing strength and is selected automatically on a small proxy
subset, and we introduce a complementary $\varepsilon$-smoothed variant
(ES-LWCE) that adds a small constant to the logarithmic denominator, providing an
explicit, user-controlled cap on the per-class weight and keeping it well defined
even for vanishing class counts, such as classes absent from a mini-batch. To
assess generality, we evaluate the
proposed losses across four
distinct application domains that together span a wide range of imbalance
severity and model architectures: long-tailed image classification, tabular
classification benchmarks, network intrusion detection, and text classification
with a Transformer encoder. Across these domains, LWCE and PLWCE improve
over Cross-Entropy as the imbalance grows and, unlike the competing re-weighting
losses, avoid the severe degradation below plain Cross-Entropy that these
baselines occasionally exhibit. These
results indicate that logarithmic weight compression offers a simple, stable, and
parameter-efficient alternative to existing re-weighting strategies for
class-imbalanced classification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Across a wide range of real-world classification tasks---including medical
diagnosis, financial fraud detection, network intrusion detection, and visual
recognition---the categories of interest are rarely observed in equal
proportions. Instead, a small number of majority categories concentrate the
overwhelming majority of the available samples, while many minority categories
are represented by only a handful of examples~\citep{he2009,johnson2019}. This
long-tailed structure is not an artifact of poor data collection but an intrinsic
property of the underlying phenomena: healthy patients vastly outnumber those
with a rare disease, legitimate transactions dwarf fraudulent ones, and benign
network traffic far exceeds malicious traffic. Because the minority categories
are often precisely the ones that carry the greatest practical cost when missed,
building classifiers that remain reliable under imbalance is a problem of broad
and lasting importance.

The central difficulty is that the standard training objective is itself biased
toward the majority. When a model is trained with ordinary Cross-Entropy (CE)
loss, the aggregate gradient signal is dominated by the numerically abundant
classes, so the optimizer can drive the overall loss down while largely ignoring
rare categories. The resulting classifier attains a high overall accuracy yet
performs poorly on exactly the minority classes that matter most---a failure mode
commonly described as majority bias. Standard accuracy therefore masks the
problem rather than revealing it, and a method that addresses imbalance must
improve minority-class performance without sacrificing the majority.

Existing remedies fall broadly into two families. Resampling methods reshape the
training distribution directly: oversampling techniques such as the Synthetic
Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) synthesize new minority
instances~\citep{chawla2002}, while undersampling discards majority examples to
restore balance. Both directions carry well-known risks---oversampling can
introduce synthetic artifacts and encourage overfitting, whereas undersampling
discards potentially informative majority data~\citep{drummond2003}. Loss-level
methods instead modify the training objective. These include margin-based losses
that enlarge the decision boundary around minority
classes~\citep{cao2019}, logit-adjustment techniques that calibrate class priors,
and loss re-weighting methods that scale the per-class loss by class-dependent
weights. Among these, re-weighting is especially attractive for three practical
reasons. Unlike resampling, it leaves the training data untouched and therefore
introduces neither synthetic artifacts nor information loss; unlike
architecture-level remedies, it adds no parameters and can be dropped into
virtually any network without altering the model; and it requires only a minimal,
modality-independent change to the loss function. Indeed, although many
sophisticated long-tailed learning methods have since been proposed, re-weighting
remains one of the few approaches that can be applied to almost any architecture
without modifying the model itself. For these reasons this paper focuses on the
re-weighting family.

Despite its appeal, re-weighting remains unsatisfying in practice. Weighted
Cross-Entropy (WCE), the most direct re-weighting scheme, assigns each class a
weight inversely proportional to its frequency,
\begin{equation}
w_c^{\mathrm{WCE}} \propto \frac{1}{n_c}.
\end{equation}
As the minority count decreases relative to the majority count, inverse-frequency
weighting induces an increasingly large rare-to-frequent weight ratio, which can
cause minority samples to dominate the class-dependent component of the gradient
scale and destabilize optimization under extreme imbalance. More sophisticated
alternatives were designed to soften this behavior but bring their own drawbacks.
Focal Loss reduces the influence of easy, typically majority, examples through a
tunable focusing term, but it operates on per-sample confidence rather than class
frequency, and its behavior can be sensitive to the focusing parameter and the
imbalance level. Class-Balanced (CB) Loss re-weights by an effective number of
samples governed by an additional hyperparameter whose effect can be sensitive to
the dataset. These methods are effective in many settings, but they alleviate the
instability through additional mechanisms rather than by directly controlling the
rare-to-frequent weight ratio while preserving the simplicity of WCE. In short, the
field still lacks a re-weighting loss that is simultaneously simple,
hyperparameter-light, numerically stable, and dependable across both the level of
imbalance and the application domain.

To address this gap, we propose Log-Weighted Cross-Entropy (LWCE). The
key idea is to replace the inverse-frequency weight of WCE with a logarithmically
compressed counterpart, applying a logarithm to the class count before inverting
it. This preserves a meaningful emphasis on rare classes---the weight still
decreases with class size---but fundamentally bounds how fast that emphasis can
grow, so the explosive behavior of WCE is removed. Crucially, the resulting loss
introduces no additional hyperparameters and can be used as a drop-in replacement
for Cross-Entropy. We further develop LWCE in two complementary directions. The
first, Power-Log Weighted Cross-Entropy (PLWCE), controls \emph{how strongly}
rare classes are emphasized: it introduces a single exponent that continuously
adjusts the focusing strength, ranging from near-uniform weighting to an
aggressive emphasis on the tail. This exponent is the only free parameter, and
it is selected automatically through a lightweight grid search on a small proxy
subset, adding negligible computational overhead. The second,
$\varepsilon$-Smoothed Log-Weighted Cross-Entropy (ES-LWCE), instead adds a small
constant $\varepsilon$ to the logarithmic denominator and reduces to LWCE as
$\varepsilon \to 0$. The base LWCE weight is already bounded for every class that
appears at least once, so the role of ES-LWCE is not to make an otherwise unbounded
weight finite, but to provide an \emph{explicit, user-controlled} cap of
$1/\varepsilon$ on the per-class weight and to keep it well defined in the
degenerate case where a class is temporarily absent---for example, when class
frequencies are estimated per mini-batch or maintained online for streaming or
growing label sets---without changing the relative ordering of class weights. It is
therefore best viewed as a robustness extension for such vanishing- or
drifting-count settings rather than as a separate competitor on datasets with
fixed, strictly positive counts. The two extensions are otherwise orthogonal: PLWCE
adjusts the \emph{strength} of the re-weighting, whereas ES-LWCE controls its
\emph{maximum weight} and smooths the extreme tail. Although the two can in
principle be combined into a single power-log-smoothed weight, we study them
separately to isolate the effect of focusing strength from that of smoothing.

We assess the proposed losses through a deliberately broad empirical study rather
than a single benchmark. To test whether the benefit of logarithmic compression
generalizes, we evaluate across three application domains that together cover a
wide spectrum of imbalance severity: long-tailed image classification on standard
vision benchmarks, a collection of tabular classification datasets drawn from
public repositories, and several network intrusion detection datasets in which
rare attack types are extremely scarce. In every domain we compare the same set
of re-weighting losses under a controlled protocol with repeated runs, and we
report metrics that explicitly reflect minority-class performance rather than
overall accuracy alone. This cross-domain design lets us examine not only whether
the proposed losses help, but how their advantage changes as imbalance becomes
more extreme.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We propose Log-Weighted Cross-Entropy (LWCE), a parameter-free drop-in
        replacement for Weighted Cross-Entropy that substitutes the
        inverse-frequency weight with a logarithmically compressed one, reducing
        the dynamic range of the class weights and the resulting optimization
        instability while retaining a meaningful emphasis on rare classes.
  \item We develop two complementary extensions of LWCE: Power-Log Weighted
        Cross-Entropy (PLWCE), whose single exponent---tuned automatically on a
        small proxy subset---controls the focusing strength, and
        $\varepsilon$-Smoothed Log-Weighted Cross-Entropy (ES-LWCE), whose smoothing
        constant imposes an explicit, user-controlled cap on the per-class weight
        and keeps it well defined even for vanishing or mini-batch-level class
        counts.
  \item We analyze the theoretical properties of the proposed weighting functions,
        showing that the weights are bounded for positive class counts, that ES-LWCE
        remains well defined even at vanishing counts, and that their rare-to-frequent
        weight ratios grow logarithmically (LWCE) or polylogarithmically (PLWCE)
        rather than linearly with the imbalance ratio.
  \item We provide a controlled cross-domain evaluation spanning image, tabular,
        network-intrusion, and text classification data, showing that the proposed
        losses improve over Cross-Entropy as imbalance grows and, unlike
        Class-Balanced Loss and Focal Loss, avoid the severe degradation below plain
        Cross-Entropy that these baselines occasionally exhibit.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Methods for learning under class imbalance can be organized by the level at which
the imbalance is addressed: the \emph{data}, by reshaping the training
distribution; the \emph{loss}, by making the training objective cost-sensitive;
the \emph{representation}, by rebalancing the learned feature space or classifier;
and the \emph{architecture}, by restructuring the model
itself~\citep{he2009,johnson2019,krawczyk2016,branco2016,zhang2023,chen2024}.
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} summarizes this taxonomy with representative methods. Our
proposal is a loss-level method and, more specifically, a frequency-based
re-weighting loss. We review all four levels for context and then position
logarithmic re-weighting among existing cost-sensitive losses.

\subsection{Data-level resampling}
Resampling rebalances the class distribution before or during training.
Oversampling synthesizes additional minority instances---the Synthetic Minority
Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) interpolates between neighbouring minority
samples~\citep{chawla2002}, and ADASYN adapts the amount of synthesis to local
difficulty~\citep{he2008}---whereas undersampling discards majority examples and
can outperform oversampling on classical learners~\citep{drummond2003}. Although
effective and classifier-agnostic, synthetic oversampling may introduce noisy or
unrealistic samples and encourage overfitting, while undersampling discards
potentially informative data~\citep{buda2018,branco2016}. These trade-offs
motivate objective-level alternatives that leave the data untouched.

\subsection{Cost-sensitive and re-weighting losses}
Algorithm-level methods instead make the loss itself imbalance-aware. We group
them by the signal that drives the re-weighting into frequency-based,
difficulty-based, and margin- or logit-based methods.
\emph{Frequency-based} methods scale each class loss by a function of its count.
The most direct instance is Weighted Cross-Entropy, which sets $w_c \propto 1/n_c$
so that the weight is inversely proportional to class count~\citep{wang2019};
these weights, however, produce a rare-to-frequent ratio that grows linearly with
the imbalance ratio and can destabilize optimization, and the benefit of such
importance weighting is known to
diminish for over-parameterized networks driven to low training
error~\citep{byrd2019}. Class-Balanced Loss softens this by weighting with an
effective number of samples, $w_c \propto (1-\beta)/(1-\beta^{n_c})$, but this
introduces an extra hyperparameter $\beta \in [0,1)$~\citep{cui2019}.
\emph{Difficulty-based} methods modulate each example by its own hardness rather
than by its class: Focal Loss down-weights easy, mostly majority, samples by a factor
$(1-p_t)^{\gamma}$, where $p_t$ is the predicted probability of the true class and
$\gamma$ a tunable focusing exponent~\citep{lin2017}, and the Gradient Harmonizing
Mechanism flattens the distribution of gradient norms so that both easy samples
and hard outliers are suppressed~\citep{li2019ghm}.
\emph{Margin- and logit-based} methods reshape the decision geometry rather than
the per-class loss magnitude: they enlarge minority margins~\citep{cao2019},
equalize gradients across frequencies~\citep{tan2020}, adjust logits by class
priors~\citep{menon2021}, or learn the weights themselves through meta-learning
and cost-sensitive representation learning~\citep{ren2018,ren2020,khan2018}, with
recent work casting weight learning as an optimal-transport problem between the
imbalanced and a balanced distribution~\citep{guo2022}.
These losses improve over plain Cross-Entropy in many settings, but they
introduce additional, often sensitive, hyperparameters and can behave
inconsistently across datasets. Our logarithmic family belongs to the
frequency-based group and is therefore complementary to the difficulty- and
margin-based axes; it targets the central weakness of frequency-based
re-weighting by bounding the frequency weight without introducing sensitive
hyperparameters.

\subsection{Representation learning and decoupling}
A complementary line of work argues that imbalance harms the classifier more
than the feature extractor. Decoupling representation and classifier
learning~\citep{kang2020} and distribution alignment~\citep{zhang2021} rebalance
only at the classification stage, while open long-tailed recognition addresses
imbalance jointly with open-set and few-shot effects~\citep{liu2019}; recent
surveys provide a broad taxonomy of these directions~\citep{zhang2023}. This concern
extends to the era of large pretrained and foundation models, where the
fine-tuning strategy itself interacts with the long tail and must be adapted to
avoid harming the rarest classes~\citep{shi2024}. Such methods are largely
orthogonal to the choice of loss and can be combined with re-weighting, so our
contribution---confined to the loss term---composes with them rather than
competing.

\subsection{Architecture-level and multi-expert methods}
A third family addresses imbalance by restructuring the network rather than the
data or the loss. Multi-branch designs train separate pathways for the head and
tail of the distribution and fuse them through cumulative, bilateral
learning~\citep{zhou2020}. Mixture-of-experts and multi-expert approaches extend
this idea by training several diverse, distribution-aware experts and then
routing or aggregating their predictions: RIDE routes inputs to complementary
experts~\citep{wang2021}, ACE assembles ally-complementary experts in a single
stage~\citep{cai2021}, LFME distils knowledge from multiple experts trained on
balanced subsets~\citep{xiang2020}, and test-agnostic aggregation adapts the
expert ensemble to an unknown test distribution~\citep{zhang2022}. Such designs
can deliver strong tail accuracy, but they increase parameter count and inference
cost, and---like the resampling and representation methods above---they are
orthogonal to the training objective: a re-weighting loss such as ours can be
applied within each branch or expert rather than replacing the architecture.

\subsection{Positioning of the proposed losses}
Within the re-weighting family, existing schemes face a dilemma: inverse-frequency
weighting is parameter-free but its rare-to-frequent weight ratio grows without
control, whereas more stable variants regain control only by adding sensitive
hyperparameters~\citep{lin2017,cui2019}. Logarithmic compression offers a middle
ground that, to our knowledge, has not been systematically studied as a
re-weighting principle across application domains. LWCE realizes it without any
additional hyperparameter, PLWCE adds a single exponent that tunes the focusing
strength, and ES-LWCE adds an $\varepsilon$ term that keeps the weight bounded and
well defined for the rarest classes. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} contrasts these
properties with the main cost-sensitive baselines: WCE is parameter-free but its
rare-to-frequent weight ratio is uncontrolled, Focal and CB Loss regain stability
only at the cost of an extra hyperparameter, whereas LWCE is a frequency-based,
parameter-free scheme whose weight ratio stays within a controlled range. We
evaluate this family against the representative cost-sensitive losses above under a
unified protocol spanning image, tabular, network-intrusion, and text
classification domains---each of which sustains its own active line of imbalance
research, from dedicated tabular benchmarks~\citep{climb2025} to intrusion
detection under severe class skew~\citep{abdelkhalek2023}.

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:method}

We study a single family of re-weighting losses built on logarithmic weight
compression. All three members replace the inverse-frequency weight of WCE with
a logarithmically compressed counterpart and differ only in how that base weight
is subsequently shaped. The three formulations and the distinct role of each are
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:losses} and defined precisely below.

\subsection{The logarithmic re-weighting family}
Consider a classification problem with $C$ classes, where class $c$ contains $n_c$
training samples. All three losses re-weight the standard cross-entropy objective
by a class-dependent factor $w_c$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-ce}
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{y_i}\,\log p_{i,y_i},
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,y_i}$ is the predicted probability of the true class $y_i$ of sample
$i$ and $N$ is the number of samples; the members differ only in the choice of
$w_c$. Log-Weighted Cross-Entropy (LWCE) uses the parameter-free weight
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lwce}
w_c^{\mathrm{LWCE}} = \frac{1}{\log(1+n_c)}.
\end{equation}
Power-Log Weighted Cross-Entropy (PLWCE) raises the logarithmic denominator to a
single exponent $\alpha>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plwce}
w_c^{\mathrm{PLWCE}} = \frac{1}{\big(\log(1+n_c)\big)^{\alpha}},
\end{equation}
which continuously tunes how strongly rare classes are emphasized and reduces to
LWCE at $\alpha=1$. $\varepsilon$-Smoothed Log-Weighted Cross-Entropy (ES-LWCE)
adds a small constant $\varepsilon>0$ to the denominator,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eslwce}
w_c^{\mathrm{ES\text{-}LWCE}} = \frac{1}{\log(1+n_c)+\varepsilon},
\end{equation}
which caps the weight at $1/\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{Weight normalization.}
In all three formulations the raw weights are mean-normalized before use,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalization}
\widetilde{w}_c = \frac{C\,w_c}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} w_j},
\end{equation}
so that $\frac{1}{C}\sum_c \widetilde{w}_c = 1$ and the overall loss scale is
independent of the weighting scheme; only the \emph{relative} emphasis across
classes differs. We apply the identical normalization to every re-weighting
baseline, including WCE and Class-Balanced loss, so that all methods share one
scale convention and differences in performance cannot be attributed to a global
change in effective learning rate. All properties below are stated for the raw
weights $w_c$; since Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization} is a single positive rescaling
common to every class, it leaves the rare-to-frequent \emph{ratios} of
Propositions~3 and~4 unchanged.

\subsection{Theoretical properties}
We now make precise the sense in which logarithmic compression tames the weight
explosion of WCE. Throughout, let $\rho = n_{\max}/n_{\min} \ge 1$ denote the
imbalance ratio between the most and least frequent classes, and let the
subscripts $\min$ and $\max$ index the rarest and the most frequent class,
respectively. Four properties characterize the proposed family.

\paragraph{Proposition 1 (Monotonicity).}
For LWCE, PLWCE, and ES-LWCE, the weight $w_c$ is strictly decreasing in the class
count $n_c$. Consequently a rarer class always receives a larger weight, which
preserves the qualitative behaviour of inverse-frequency weighting.
\emph{Proof.} $\log(1+n_c)$ is strictly increasing in $n_c$, so its reciprocal,
any positive power of its reciprocal, and the reciprocal of its $\varepsilon$-shift
are all strictly decreasing in $n_c$. $\hfill\square$
Because mean normalization (Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization}) multiplies every class
weight by the same positive constant, it preserves this ordering, so the normalized
weights $\widetilde{w}_c$ are also strictly decreasing in $n_c$.

\paragraph{Proposition 2 (Boundedness).}
For $n_c \ge 1$ the individual raw weights are bounded:
$0 < w_c^{\mathrm{LWCE}} \le 1/\log 2$ and, for any fixed $\alpha > 0$,
$0 < w_c^{\mathrm{PLWCE}} \le 1/(\log 2)^{\alpha}$. The ES-LWCE weight is bounded
even without this assumption: $0 < w_c^{\mathrm{ES\text{-}LWCE}} \le 1/\varepsilon$
for every $n_c \ge 0$. These bounds concern the raw weights $w_c$; the normalized
weights $\widetilde{w}_c$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization} depend on the class
composition and are not claimed to satisfy the same fixed bound.
\emph{Proof.} Each weight is maximized at its smallest admissible count by
Proposition~1. For LWCE and PLWCE this is $n_c = 1$, giving $1/\log 2$ and
$1/(\log 2)^{\alpha}$. For ES-LWCE the denominator is at least $\varepsilon$,
attained as $n_c \to 0$, so the weight never exceeds $1/\varepsilon$.
$\hfill\square$

This proposition also clarifies the distinct role of ES-LWCE. LWCE is already
bounded for every class that appears at least once; what ES-LWCE adds is
robustness at $n_c = 0$, where the LWCE denominator $\log(1+n_c)$ vanishes and the
weight diverges. Such vanishing counts arise, for instance, when class frequencies
are estimated per mini-batch. ES-LWCE turns the implicit bound of LWCE into an
explicit, user-controlled cap of $1/\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{Proposition 3 (Dynamic-range compression).}
Fix the minority count $n_{\min} \ge 1$ and let $\rho = n_{\max}/n_{\min} \to \infty$
through growth of $n_{\max}$. The rare-to-frequent weight ratio then grows
\emph{linearly} in $\rho$ under WCE but only \emph{logarithmically} for LWCE and
\emph{polylogarithmically} for PLWCE. Under WCE,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratio-wce}
\frac{w_{\min}^{\mathrm{WCE}}}{w_{\max}^{\mathrm{WCE}}}
   = \frac{n_{\max}}{n_{\min}} = \rho,
\end{equation}
whereas under LWCE and PLWCE,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratio-lwce}
\frac{w_{\min}^{\mathrm{LWCE}}}{w_{\max}^{\mathrm{LWCE}}}
   = \frac{\log(1+n_{\max})}{\log(1+n_{\min})} = O(\log \rho),
\qquad
\frac{w_{\min}^{\mathrm{PLWCE}}}{w_{\max}^{\mathrm{PLWCE}}}
   = \left(\frac{\log(1+n_{\max})}{\log(1+n_{\min})}\right)^{\!\alpha}
   = O\big((\log \rho)^{\alpha}\big).
\end{equation}
Here $O(\cdot)$ denotes the asymptotic growth order in the imbalance ratio: for
positive functions $f$ and $g$, $f = O(g)$ means that $f$ grows no faster than a
constant multiple of $g$ as $\rho \to \infty$. Comparing
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ratio-wce} and~\eqref{eq:ratio-lwce}, the compression reduces the
dynamic range of the weights from linear in $\rho$ (WCE) to \emph{logarithmic} for
LWCE and \emph{polylogarithmic}, $O((\log\rho)^{\alpha})$, for PLWCE, while
preserving the ordering that favours rare classes. Note that this is a statement
about the \emph{ratio}: unlike the individual weights of Proposition~2, the ratio
is not constant-bounded but grows without limit as $\rho \to \infty$---only much
more slowly than the linear growth of WCE. For PLWCE with a large exponent
$\alpha$ the polylogarithmic ratio can itself become sizeable, so the compression
is strongest for LWCE and is moderated as $\alpha$ increases.
Figure~\ref{fig:weight-compression} makes this compression concrete: with the
majority count fixed at $n_{\max}=10^4$, the rarest class receives a weight
$\rho = 10^4$ times larger than the majority under WCE, but only about $13$ times
larger under LWCE, with PLWCE and ES-LWCE interpolating between these regimes.

\paragraph{Proposition 4 (Gradient-scale control).}
In weighted cross-entropy the per-sample gradient with respect to the logits is
the unweighted cross-entropy gradient scaled by the class weight $w_{y_i}$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:weighted-ce}). The full gradient magnitude also depends on the
predicted probabilities and the feature representation, so we do not claim that
the minority-to-majority gradient ratio equals the weight ratio exactly. Rather,
the \emph{class-dependent multiplicative component} of the gradient scale follows
the same asymptotic order as the corresponding weight ratio of Proposition~3: it
is $O(\rho)$ under WCE but $O(\log\rho)$ under LWCE. Logarithmic compression
therefore limits the extent to which minority gradients can overwhelm the majority
under extreme imbalance, which is the mechanism by which LWCE mitigates the
optimization instability of WCE; ES-LWCE additionally caps the worst-case
class-dependent factor at $1/\varepsilon$. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests this
mechanism directly by tracking the per-epoch gradient norm and the empirical
minority-to-majority gradient ratio.

\subsection{Parameter selection for PLWCE}\label{sec:plwce-selection}
LWCE and ES-LWCE require essentially no tuning: LWCE is parameter-free, and the
$\varepsilon$ of ES-LWCE is a user-set cap rather than an accuracy knob
(Section~\ref{sec:eslwce-use}). PLWCE, however, exposes a genuine hyperparameter
$\alpha$ that controls the focusing strength, with three qualitatively distinct
regimes:
\begin{itemize}
\item $0 < \alpha < 1$: \emph{mild} re-weighting, weaker than LWCE;
\item $\alpha = 1$: exactly LWCE;
\item $\alpha > 1$: \emph{strong} tail emphasis.
\end{itemize}
We select $\alpha$ with a reproducible, data-driven procedure that never touches
the test set. For each dataset (and each imbalance level, where applicable) we
draw a stratified \emph{proxy} subset from the \emph{training} split---a fixed
fraction of the training data, with a minimum number of samples per class so that
rare classes are never eliminated from the proxy---and hold out a validation
portion of that proxy. A model is trained on the proxy for a reduced number of
epochs at each candidate $\alpha$ on a fixed grid, and we retain the value that
maximizes the macro-averaged $F_1$ on the proxy validation set. Only this single
scalar is then used to train the final model under the full protocol. The search
is therefore confined to the training split, is run once per dataset, and adds a
bounded, one-time cost (the number of grid points times the reduced-epoch proxy
budget) on top of a single full run; it introduces no per-iteration overhead and
no test-set leakage.

\subsection{ES-LWCE and practical use cases}\label{sec:eslwce-use}
ES-LWCE is not intended to outperform LWCE on datasets whose class counts are
fixed and strictly positive; there, LWCE is already bounded (Proposition~2) and
the two coincide as $\varepsilon \to 0$. Its value appears when the class counts
used to form the weights can \emph{vanish or drift}. When frequencies are
estimated per mini-batch, or maintained online for streaming or continually
growing label sets, a class may be momentarily absent ($n_c = 0$); the LWCE weight
then diverges, whereas ES-LWCE remains well defined with raw weight at most
$1/\varepsilon$ \emph{before} the mean normalization of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization}. The constant $\varepsilon$ trades off two effects:
\begin{itemize}
\item small $\varepsilon$: stronger minority emphasis, closer to LWCE;
\item large $\varepsilon$: more conservative gradient scaling, closer to uniform.
\end{itemize}
As $\varepsilon \to \infty$ the raw weights all vanish, but after mean
normalization they converge to the uniform (Cross-Entropy) weighting; thus, on the
normalized weights actually used in training, $\varepsilon$ shifts the emphasis
monotonically from the LWCE limit toward the uniform limit and never past LWCE, so
it acts purely as a smoothing/robustness control. We therefore position ES-LWCE as an extension for
vanishing- or batch-adaptive-count settings rather than as a method that is
required at the dataset level.

\subsection{Computational complexity}\label{sec:complexity}
All three losses add negligible cost over plain Cross-Entropy. The class weights
depend only on the class counts and are precomputed once from the training split;
there are no additional model parameters and no extra backward-pass terms. Once
the weights are precomputed, LWCE and ES-LWCE incur the same asymptotic training
complexity as WCE, requiring only a scalar multiplication per sample. The only
added cost specific to PLWCE is the one-time grid search of
Section~\ref{sec:plwce-selection}, which is bounded and performed on a reduced
proxy rather than at full training scale.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
Our experiments are organized around four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Does the advantage of the logarithmic family grow as the imbalance becomes
      more severe?
\item Does LWCE empirically reduce the optimization instability of WCE?
\item Does the PLWCE exponent $\alpha$ control tail emphasis as intended?
\item Is ES-LWCE stable under vanishing or extremely rare class counts?
\end{enumerate}
To probe architecture- and modality-independence, we evaluate the same loss family
across image classification (CIFAR-10/100-LT with a ResNet-32 backbone), tabular
classification and network-intrusion detection (multilayer perceptrons), and text
classification (a compact Transformer encoder on a long-tailed emotion benchmark).

\subsection{Datasets and imbalance construction}\label{sec:datasets}
We evaluate on four domains spanning a wide range of imbalance severity, from mildly
skewed tabular benchmarks ($\rho\!\approx\!2$) to extreme network-intrusion data
($\rho$ up to $1.7\times10^{4}$); Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists all datasets. For
the two image benchmarks we construct long-tailed (LT) versions of CIFAR-10 and
CIFAR-100 by subsampling each class to an exponential profile,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lt-profile}
n_c = n_{\max}\,\rho^{-\frac{c-1}{C-1}}, \qquad c = 1,\ldots,C,
\end{equation}
with $n_{\max}=5000$ (CIFAR-10) and $500$ (CIFAR-100), and sweep the imbalance ratio
$\rho\in\{10,50,100\}$; the standard test sets are left balanced. The tabular,
network-intrusion, and text datasets are used with their \emph{natural} class
imbalance and their official or standard train/test splits. GoEmotions is filtered
to single-label examples, giving a $28$-class long-tailed text benchmark.

\subsection{Models and training protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
Because the study compares losses rather than architectures, within each domain the
backbone and the full training protocol are held identical across all losses; only
the loss function changes. \emph{Image}: a ResNet-32 backbone trained from scratch
with SGD (learning rate $0.1$, momentum $0.9$, weight decay $2\times10^{-4}$), a
multi-step schedule decaying by $0.01$ at epochs $160$ and $180$, batch size $128$,
and standard CIFAR augmentation (random crop with $4$-pixel padding and horizontal
flip) for $200$ epochs. \emph{Tabular} and \emph{network intrusion}: a multilayer
perceptron ($256$--$128$--$64$ with batch normalization, ReLU, and dropout $0.3$)
trained with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) and cosine
annealing to $10^{-5}$, batch size $512$, for $100$ epochs (tabular) and $50$ epochs
(network). \emph{Text}: a compact Transformer encoder ($d_{\mathrm{model}}=128$,
$4$ heads, $4$ layers, feed-forward width $256$, masked mean pooling) trained from
scratch with AdamW (learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$), a
linear warmup over three epochs followed by cosine decay, gradient-norm clipping at
$1.0$, and batch size $64$, for $50$ epochs. Every configuration is run with five
random seeds ($42$--$46$), and we report the mean and standard deviation. All losses
use the mean-normalized weights of Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization}, including the WCE
and Class-Balanced baselines, so that every method shares one scale convention.

\subsection{Baselines and evaluation}\label{sec:setup}
We compare the three proposed losses (LWCE, PLWCE, ES-LWCE) against Cross-Entropy,
Weighted Cross-Entropy, an inverse-square-root variant, Class-Balanced
loss~\citep{cui2019}, Focal loss~\citep{lin2017}, and Logit
Adjustment~\citep{menon2021} as a margin/posterior-shift baseline. Because overall
accuracy is uninformative under strong imbalance, we report macro-averaged $F_1$
and worst-class accuracy as the primary metrics, and additionally balanced
accuracy, the geometric mean of per-class recall, minority-class recall, and the
full per-class accuracy vector. All results are averaged over five random seeds and
reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation.

\subsection{Hyperparameter selection}\label{sec:hyperparam}
A recurring concern with re-weighting losses is that any apparent advantage may
stem from tuning rather than from the weighting scheme itself. To rule this out,
every baseline that exposes a free parameter is tuned under an identical, automated
budget, while LWCE is used exactly as defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lwce} with \emph{no}
search at all. Concretely, the PLWCE and inverse-power exponents, the Focal
focusing parameter, the ES-LWCE smoothing constant, and the Logit-Adjustment
temperature are each selected on a validation split by the procedure of
Section~\ref{sec:plwce-selection}; Cross-Entropy, WCE, the inverse-square-root
variant, and Class-Balanced loss are parameter-free or use their authors'
recommended value. Search uses a deterministic grid over each parameter (with the
Focal grid lower-bounded at $\gamma = 1$, below which the modulating factor
$(1-p_t)^{\gamma}$ yields an unstable gradient as $p_t \to 1$), so that the entire
admissible range is covered reproducibly, and the smoothing constant $\varepsilon$
is searched on a logarithmic grid because its effect on the weights is
multiplicative. LWCE therefore competes with no tuning against baselines that each
receive a full search.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} summarizes the primary metric, macro-averaged $F_1$, aggregated
within each domain, together with the mean rank of each loss over all $26$
dataset-and-imbalance settings. Two patterns are consistent across domains. First,
the logarithmic re-weighting family occupies the top of the overall ranking: LWCE
attains the best mean rank ($4.08$), and the proposed losses together with the
inverse-power and inverse-square-root variants form a leading cluster well ahead of
plain Cross-Entropy (mean rank $6.69$). Second, unbounded inverse-frequency
weighting is the weakest method overall: WCE has the worst mean rank ($8.19$) and
Class-Balanced loss the second worst ($7.62$), consistent with the weight-explosion
mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:method}---as the imbalance grows, the uncontrolled
weight ratio of WCE degrades its accuracy below even Cross-Entropy.

No single loss dominates every domain, which is expected given how differently the
domains are structured: logit adjustment is strongest on the balanced-test image
benchmarks, LWCE on the tabular datasets, PLWCE on the long-tailed text data, and
the combined variant---by a small margin---on network intrusion. The logarithmic
family is nonetheless consistently near the top in all four, which is the central
claim of a domain-independent re-weighting principle. On worst-class accuracy and
the geometric mean of per-class recall the picture is more nuanced. WCE and CB
often achieve the highest tail recall (for example, the highest geometric mean on
the tabular datasets), but only by sacrificing head accuracy, so their macro-$F_1$
falls to the bottom of the table; the proposed losses instead improve tail
performance while preserving overall accuracy. This trade-off is most visible on the
$28$-class text benchmark, where PLWCE reaches a geometric mean of $0.240$ against
$0.073$ for Cross-Entropy while also achieving the best macro-$F_1$, whereas the
higher tail recall of CB comes with the lowest overall accuracy. Full per-dataset
tables with all metrics are provided in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Optimization stability and mechanism}\label{sec:stability}
Better macro-$F_1$ alone does not establish that the logarithmic family stabilizes
optimization. We therefore verify the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:method} directly, using the most severe image setting
(CIFAR-100-LT at $\rho=100$, $100$ classes) as a representative case and treating
optimization stability operationally through three quantities: the dynamic range of
the class weights actually used in training, the minority-to-majority gradient
ratio, and the smoothness and magnitude of the training-loss and gradient-norm
curves.

\paragraph{Weight distribution (Proposition~3).}
On the mean-normalized weights $\widetilde{w}_c$ used in training, the empirical
dynamic range matches the theory closely. The largest-to-smallest normalized weight
ratio is $100$ for WCE and $97$ for CB (coefficient of variation $\approx 1.2$),
$10$ for the inverse-square-root variant and $8.4$ for the inverse-power variant,
but only $3.5$ for LWCE, $3.0$ for PLWCE, and $2.4$ for ES-LWCE (coefficient of
variation $0.27$--$0.39$). The logarithmic family thus compresses the weight
dynamic range by more than an order of magnitude relative to WCE, exactly as
predicted by Proposition~3.

\paragraph{Gradient dynamics.}
Figure~\ref{fig:gradient-mechanism} tracks the per-epoch training loss, parameter
gradient norm, and minority-to-majority gradient ratio $R_G$. The gradient ratio
(panel~c) is where the mechanism is clearest: at the start of training WCE and CB
amplify the minority-class gradient by a factor of about $21$ relative to the
majority, whereas LWCE and PLWCE keep this factor near $2$--$3$ and plain CE near
$1$. This early amplification is exactly the class-dependent gradient scaling of
Proposition~4, and it coincides with a slower, noisier decrease of the training
loss (panel~a) and a higher gradient norm (panel~b, roughly $0.36$ for WCE and CB
versus $0.16$ for LWCE and PLWCE at the end of training) for the unbounded losses.
Note that the amplification is a \emph{transient} of the early phase: strong
re-weighting eventually overfits the few minority training samples, driving their
gradients toward zero, so the WCE ratio falls below one late in training. It is the
instability during the critical early epochs---not the converged state---that the
logarithmic compression removes, and this is consistent with WCE and CB ranking at
the bottom of Table~\ref{tab:main} despite their re-weighting intent.

\subsection{Ablation and sensitivity}\label{sec:ablation}
We isolate the contribution of each ingredient of the logarithmic family---the
logarithmic compression itself, the power exponent $\alpha$, and the smoothing
constant $\varepsilon$---through the ablation of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. Reading
from WCE (no compression) to LWCE isolates the effect of logarithmic compression;
adding $\alpha$ gives PLWCE and adding $\varepsilon$ gives ES-LWCE; the
\emph{Combined} variant activates both extensions at once,
$w_c \propto (\log(1+n_c)+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}$, and is included---following the
same design as the other rows---to probe whether the two extensions are worth
merging into a single loss.

The ablation shows that almost all of the benefit comes from the logarithmic
compression itself. Averaged over all $26$ dataset-and-imbalance settings, replacing
the inverse-frequency weight of WCE with the logarithmic weight of LWCE raises
macro-$F_1$ from $0.586$ to $0.625$, whereas adding the power exponent (PLWCE,
$0.623$), the smoothing constant (ES-LWCE, $0.624$), or both (Combined, $0.624$)
leaves the aggregate essentially unchanged. The exponent $\alpha$ is not
uniformly helpful: it improves the aggregate on the image and text data but not on
the tabular datasets, so its effect is dataset-dependent rather than universal, and
its value is precisely what the selection procedure of
Section~\ref{sec:plwce-selection} is designed to recover per dataset.

We do not propose the Combined variant as a method. It yields no improvement over
PLWCE or ES-LWCE alone: the selection procedure consistently drives $\varepsilon$ to
the lower end of its grid, so the Combined weight collapses back onto PLWCE. This is
a useful negative result rather than a failure---it shows that, on datasets with
fixed and strictly positive class counts, the smoothing dimension is inactive for
accuracy, exactly as anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:eslwce-use}: $\varepsilon$ is a
robustness control for vanishing or drifting counts, not an accuracy lever. It
therefore supports treating PLWCE and ES-LWCE as separate tools rather than a single
combined loss.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}
To test whether the ranking of Section~\ref{sec:results} is statistically
meaningful, we treat each of the $26$ dataset-and-imbalance settings as a block,
average over seeds within each block, and compare the ten losses on macro-$F_1$ as
the primary endpoint. A Friedman test rejects the null hypothesis that the losses
are equivalent ($\chi^2 = 60.4$, $\mathrm{df}=9$, $p = 1.1\times10^{-9}$). For the
post-hoc analysis we take the best-ranked loss, LWCE, as the control and compare it
against each other loss with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the $26$ paired
scores, applying a Holm correction for the nine comparisons;
Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports the adjusted $p$-values together with the mean ranks
and the win/tie/loss counts.

LWCE significantly outperforms the four standard baselines---plain Cross-Entropy,
WCE, Class-Balanced loss, and Focal loss---at the $5\%$ level after correction
(winning, for example, $24$ of $26$ settings against Cross-Entropy and $20$ of $26$
against WCE). In particular the method that our theory targets, WCE, is
significantly beaten, in line with the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:stability}. The
remaining strong re-weighting variants (the inverse-power and inverse-square-root
losses, PLWCE, ES-LWCE, and logit adjustment) form a statistical tie with LWCE, with
adjusted $p$-values of $1.0$; we therefore do not claim that LWCE is uniformly the
single best loss, only that the logarithmic family sits firmly in the top tier and
significantly improves on the commonly used baselines while requiring no tuning.
